Incoterms 2020 in practice

Incoterms allocate three things between a buyer and seller: cost, risk, and obligation. They do not allocate title, and they are not law — they apply only because a contract says they do.

For anyone arranging the movement, the term decides one practical question above the rest: who chooses, and who pays, the freight forwarder.

What changed in the 2020 revision

  • DAT became DPU (Delivered at Place Unloaded), removing the restriction to a "terminal."
  • CIP now requires all-risks insurance cover; CIF still requires only the minimum cover — a recurring source of under-insurance disputes when the two are confused.
  • FCA gained an on-board notation option, letting the buyer instruct the carrier to issue a bill of lading with an on-board notation to the seller — solving a long-standing problem for FCA sellers who need an on-board bill for a letter of credit.
  • Own-means-of-transport is explicitly recognised under FCA, DAP, DPU and DDP.

Six recurring errors

  1. Using FOB/CIF/CFR for containerised cargo. These terms were designed for bulk cargo loaded over a ship's rail. For containers handed over at a CFS or terminal days before loading, the seller retains risk during a period it does not control. FCA, CPT and CIP are the terms actually designed for containers — though FOB and CIF remain in near-universal use in Indian trade regardless.
  2. Naming no place. "CIF" alone is meaningless. It must specify a destination: "CIF Rotterdam, Incoterms® 2020."
  3. EXW for exports. Under EXW the buyer is responsible for export clearance — but a foreign buyer usually cannot file an Indian shipping bill or claim under the exporter's IEC. In practice the seller ends up doing it anyway, off-contract and unprotected. FCA is the safer default.
  4. DDP into an unfamiliar country. The seller becomes an importer of record somewhere it may not be able to register for VAT or claim input credit. A DDP quote into a new market should carry a large risk premium and an explicit carve-out for destination taxes.
  5. Assuming the Incoterm sets payment terms. It does not. CIF says nothing about when the buyer pays.
  6. Assuming the Incoterm transfers title. It does not. Title passes according to the sale contract and the applicable law, separately from the Incoterm.

Most Indian exporters ship FOB because the overseas buyer insists on it — precisely because controlling the freight is valuable. The freight decision, and the margin that comes with it, follows whoever holds that control.
