Shipping documents explained

An international shipment is, functionally, defined by its paperwork. What follows is what each document is for, who issues it, and what specifically tends to go wrong with it.

Commercial documents

DocumentIssued byWhat goes wrong
Commercial InvoiceSellerMissing Incoterm or HS code; value inconsistent with the letter of credit or the declared transaction value. Under-invoicing is a customs offence, not a negotiating position.
Packing ListSellerWeights inconsistent with the Verified Gross Mass or bill of lading; missing marks and numbers, which stalls examination.
Certificate of OriginChamber of commerce (non-preferential) or a DGFT-designated agency (preferential, under a trade agreement)Wrong form for the agreement being claimed; failure to meet the rules-of-origin criteria. Under CAROTAR 2020 the Indian importer must independently hold origin evidence, not just the certificate itself — see Nordic isn't EFTA on the blog for what that looks like in practice.
Insurance CertificateInsurer or brokerCover starting after loading, or a sum insured below the 110% of CIF value that letters of credit typically require.

Transport documents

DocumentIssued byKey characteristic
Master Bill of LadingShipping lineContract between the line and the booking party. A document of title.
House Bill of LadingForwarder / NVOCCContract between the forwarder and its customer. A letter of credit must expressly permit a house bill, or it will be rejected.
Sea WaybillLine or forwarderNot a document of title. Cargo releases to the named consignee on identification — fast, but only appropriate when payment is already secured.
Telex / Express ReleaseLine, on shipper's instructionOriginals are surrendered at origin so none are needed at destination. Irreversible once issued — never issue before payment.
Air WaybillAirline / forwarderNever a document of title, and non-negotiable. Cannot be used to control payment the way a negotiable bill of lading can.

A bill of lading performs three functions: receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract of carriage, and — when made "to order" and properly endorsed — document of title. That third function is what makes letter-of-credit trade possible, and why releasing cargo without an original is treated as a fundamental breach rather than an ordinary liability question.

Regulatory and customs documents

DocumentWhat it is
IECThe ten-digit, PAN-based code from DGFT required for any commercial import or export. Must be reconfirmed annually or it is deactivated — a common, avoidable cause of a shipment stopping dead.
Shipping BillThe export declaration filed on ICEGATE, carrying the Let Export Order once granted.
Bill of EntryThe import declaration — home consumption, warehousing, or ex-bond — carrying the Out of Charge order once granted.
e-Way BillRequired for the road or rail leg above a value threshold under GST. Validity is distance-based; expiry mid-transit is a routine and entirely avoidable penalty.
e-BRCElectronic Bank Realisation Certificate — evidence that export proceeds were actually realised, required for incentive claims.

Letter of credit documentation

Under a documentary credit governed by UCP 600, the bank pays against documents, not goods. A single typographical inconsistency is a discrepancy, and a discrepancy is a valid reason for the bank to refuse payment. The checks worth running on every LC shipment:

Does the credit permit a house or forwarder's bill, or does it require a carrier's bill?
Does it permit transhipment? Many prohibit it — a routing via a transhipment hub can create a discrepancy on its own.
Does it permit partial shipments?
Is the latest shipment date respected — the "on board" date on the bill of lading is what counts.
Is the goods description on the bill of lading consistent with the credit?
Are the notify party, consignee and shipper exact, character for character?
Is the number of originals as required?
Are freight terms stated as the credit requires — "Freight Prepaid" versus "Freight Collect"?
