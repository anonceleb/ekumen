Types of freight forwarders & how Indian FF is organised

"Freight forwarder" covers everyone from a two-person air cargo agent to a listed 3PL running its own fleet. What separates them is mode, the licences they hold, and where they sit in a layered network of principals and sub-agents.

Types of forwarder, by mode and function

TypeWhat it does
Ocean freight forwarderBooks and manages sea freight. Either sells carrier space directly as an agent, or operates as an NVOCC — contracting bulk slots from shipping lines and reselling them under its own House Bill of Lading, taking on carrier liability to its own customer.
Air freight forwarder / IATA cargo agentBooks air cargo space, usually accredited by IATA (or acting as a sub-agent to an accredited GSA — General Sales Agent) to issue Air Waybills directly against an airline's tariff.
Multimodal Transport Operator (MTO)Registered under the Multimodal Transportation of Goods Act, 1993 to issue a single Multimodal Transport Document covering door-to-door carriage across two or more modes under one liability regime, rather than separate bills for each leg.
Customs broker / CHANot a forwarder by function, but frequently the same company: licensed under the CBLR to file customs declarations. See CBLR and AEO explained.
Consolidator / co-loaderAggregates LCL or air cargo from multiple shippers into a single container or unit load, splitting the cost of a full load across smaller consignments.
Project cargo forwarderHandles out-of-gauge, heavy-lift or oversized cargo — machinery, turbines, plant equipment — requiring route surveys, specialised equipment and often multi-country permitting.
3PL / 4PLA third-party logistics provider bundles forwarding with warehousing, inland transport and distribution; a fourth-party provider goes a layer further and manages other providers on the shipper's behalf without owning assets itself.

Licences and registrations that structure the trade

RegistrationIssuing bodyWhat it permits
Importer-Exporter Code (IEC)DGFTBaseline registration required to import or export from India at all — every shipper, and most forwarders, hold one.
Customs Broker LicenceJurisdictional Customs Commissionerate, under CBLR 2018Authorises filing of Shipping Bills and Bills of Entry and clearing cargo through Customs. Tied to a named licensee, not the company generally.
MTO RegistrationDirector General of ShippingAuthorises issuing a Multimodal Transport Document under the MMTG Act, 1993.
IATA Cargo Agent AccreditationIATA (via its India cargo agency programme)Authorises issuing Air Waybills directly against IATA member airline tariffs.
AEO StatusCBICNot a forwarding licence but a trade-facilitation status open to forwarders, CHAs and importers alike, rewarding compliance history with faster clearance.

Associations that structure the industry

Indian freight forwarding is organised less by regulation than by a layer of trade associations that set standard trading conditions, arbitrate disputes between members, and represent the trade to government. The Federation of Freight Forwarders' Associations in India (FFFAI) is the national umbrella body, itself made up of regional associations — groupings such as the Bombay Custom House Agents' Association and equivalents in other port cities, historically organised around Customs House Agents before the CBLR nomenclature changed. Internationally, many Indian forwarders are members of FIATA, the global federation whose standard trading conditions and FBL bill of lading format underpin a large share of cross-border forwarding contracts.

How the trade is layered in practice

  • Global / national 3PLs hold carrier contracts directly, run their own branch network, and sub-contract origin or destination handling to local agents where they have no branch.
  • Regional forwarders typically hold both a forwarding operation and a CBLR licence in one or two ports, and act as destination agents for larger forwarders' shipments routed through their city.
  • Single-branch CHA-cum-forwarders are the base of the pyramid — often the actual counterparty an SME importer deals with, even when the shipment was booked through a larger forwarder's network above them.

The practical effect of this layering: the entity that quotes a shipment, the entity that physically clears it through Customs, and the entity that holds the carrier contract are frequently three different companies, connected by sub-agency agreements the shipper never sees. Asking who holds the CBLR licence for clearance, and who holds the carrier or NVOCC contract for the ocean or air leg, is usually enough to map the real chain.
