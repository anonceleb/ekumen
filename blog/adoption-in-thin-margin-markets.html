Good tooling, thin margins, and the adoption problem nobody prices in

Some of the operators we've spent time with run genuinely good software. Yard planning systems, custom-built platforms, real engineering behind them. If your mental model of this market is "legacy tools, ripe for disruption," a few conversations will correct that fast.

So the interesting question was never "does this market have tools." It's a systems question: given the tooling that already exists, why does so much of the actual work still happen by hand?

Two different constraints, often mistaken for one

It's tempting to treat "the software is dated" and "the work is done manually" as the same problem with the same fix: ship better software. They're not the same problem. One is a capability question. The other is an adoption question, and adoption runs on a completely different clock.

Capability can be solved by an engineering team in weeks. Adoption is gated by things engineering can't touch directly: whether a thin-margin operator can absorb the cost of retraining staff, whether they trust a small vendor to still be around in three years, whether the manual workaround they've already built is "good enough" that replacing it doesn't feel worth the disruption. None of that shows up in a product demo, and none of it moves at engineering speed.

Why this matters more in thin-margin markets specifically

In a high-margin business, the cost of adoption friction is a rounding error against the efficiency gained. In a thin-margin one, it's the opposite: the friction cost is often larger than the visible upside, at least in the short window an operator is willing to evaluate it over. That's not irrationality. That's correct math for the constraints they're actually operating under.

Which means the naive version of "build a great tool for this market" quietly assumes a margin structure the market doesn't have. Solve for the tooling gap alone, aimed at a segment where tooling was never the binding constraint, and the tool doesn't fail on capability. It fails on a variable the roadmap never accounted for.

What a systems view changes

Once adoption capacity is treated as its own constraint, not a downstream consequence of "the software wasn't good enough," a few things follow:

  • Sequencing matters. Where an operator's adoption capacity is genuinely low right now, the entry point is the coordination and compliance work that has to happen regardless, not a tool that asks them to change process first.
  • The segment isn't uniform. Some operators have real capacity to adopt and are simply waiting for the right partner; treating the whole tier as adoption-constrained would be its own error.
  • This is a rethink of sequencing and segmentation, not a retreat from building tools. The tooling question stays open. It's just no longer the first question.

We're not writing this off as a market that won't pay for software. It's more that the market won't pay for software pitched at the wrong constraint, and once we started looking past the tooling gap to what was actually gating the decision underneath it, a lot of the earlier confusion made more sense.
